### 1.	Module aims and learning outcomes
#### 1.1 Introduction
This module aims to provide students with the ability to study and reflect on key principles and methods in research based on the scientific method in the Computing subject areas, developing research competencies, data analysis skills, a critical design and independent learning. The module covers various tools and techniques that you, as a researcher, may need and allows for feedback throughout the module through cases and project tasks. The module also provides essential insight into the legal, social, ethical and professional issues applicable to computing projects, as well as the relevant codes of practice and practices. The two core textbooks offer a comprehensive overview of the subject, based around the scientific method and offering details for doing research in various types of organisations. You will be required to identify with research practicalities, as well as use critical analysis of the key principles of research methodology to bring together the knowledge gained throughout other modules on your programme. There will be continuous assessment throughout with case studies and milestone assessments. Hence facilitating peer-to-peer, as well as instructor interactions. You will be able to demonstrate your ability and strengths through evidence and reflections by maintaining an e-Portfolio for this module. This module's assessment has been explicitly designed to allow you to work on your project's proposal. This module ends at the moment you will start of the Project Module where you will be able to work with your allocated supervisor on the output of this Research Methods, Ethics application included.

#### 1.2 A summary of the learning outcomes of the module
•	Acquire the ability to study and reflect on key principles and methods in research based on the scientific method and relevant to various disciplines.

#### 2.2.1 Initial Post
Case Study: Accessibility in Software Development

(Sutrop et al., 2020) indicate the importance of key elements shared by disciplines and research professions that include consent, privacy, data management, fraud, coverage, accessibility, and plagiarism in a research project. The case study highlights a serious ethical failure in software development, as accessibility requirements were knowingly overlooked despite organisational policies, accessibility standards, and guidance from a Diversity Advisory Board. Although accessibility defects were identified during testing, AllTogether proceeded with the release to satisfy project deadlines and business objectives. This decision violated key principles of the Association for Computing Machinery (ACM) Code of Ethics, including promoting human well-being, avoiding harm, ensuring fairness, and preventing discrimination. As a result, users who relied on assistive technologies such as screen readers, keyboard navigation, magnification software, and alternative input devices were excluded from fully using the product.

This decision also created significant legal risks. Many countries have disability discrimination and accessibility laws that require digital products and services to be accessible. By knowingly releasing software that did not comply with recognised standards, including the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) (Gogoll et al., 2021), AllTogether increased its exposure to legal complaints, regulatory scrutiny, and potential liability arising from discriminatory outcomes.

Socially, the case study demonstrates how inaccessible technology can reinforce barriers to participation in employment, education, and community activities. Leadership comments questioning the importance of disabled users reflected a lack of inclusiveness and consideration for minority groups. Consequently, disabled users experienced frustration and exclusion, while some customers reverted to older software versions. This highlights the wider societal impact of failing to prioritise accessibility (Fiesler et al., 2020).

Computing professionals have a responsibility to exercise independent judgement and prioritise user welfare. Although the accessibility and customer support teams attempted to lessen the impact through support measures, these actions were reactive rather than preventative. A more professional approach would have been to delay the release until accessibility issues were resolved.

A comparison with the BCS Code of Conduct reinforces these concerns, as both the ACM and BCS emphasise public interest, professional competence, integrity, and accountability. 

Overall, the case study demonstrates the importance of embedding accessibility throughout the software development lifecycle to create inclusive, fair, and socially responsible technology.

#### 2.4 Unit 4: Seminar – Peer Review Activity
Title: Case Study on Privacy

Please read the case below and answer the questions. You need to determine the ethical issues involved with this case.

The Case

Ricardo works for the records department of his local government as a computer records clerk, where he has access to files of property tax records. For a scientific study, a researcher, Beth, has been granted access to the numerical portion “but not the corresponding names” of some records. Beth finds some information that she would like to use, but she needs the names and addresses corresponding with certain properties. Beth asks Ricardo to retrieve these names and addresses, so she can contact these people for more information and for permission to do further study.

Now consider, what are the ethical issues involved in deciding which of these options to pursue?

1.If Ricardo is not responsible for determining allowable access, should he release the names and addresses? 

Answer: No, Ricardo should not release the names and addresses. Ricardo should follow organisational policies and the conditions under which the information is held. If Beth requires access to identifying information, she should seek approval through the appropriate authority responsible for granting access rather than asking Ricardo to bypass established procedures.

2.Suppose Ricardo were responsible for determining allowable access to the files. What ethical issues would be involved in his deciding whether to grant access to Beth?
   
Answer: If Ricardo were responsible for granting access, he would need to balance the potential benefits of Beth's research against individuals' rights to privacy and confidentiality. He would also need to consider whether the property owners had provided informed consent for their personal information to be shared. In making his decision, Ricardo should follow legal, ethical, and organisational requirements, ensuring that any disclosure of information is properly authorised and protects the rights of the individuals involved.

3.Should Beth be allowed to contact the individuals involved? That is, should the Records department release individuals' names to a researcher? What are the ethical issues for the Records department to consider? 

Answer: Beth should only be allowed to contact individuals if their privacy is protected and proper consent or authorisation has been obtained. The Records Department must consider privacy, confidentiality, informed consent, and compliance with applicable laws and policies before releasing any personal information. A more ethical approach would be to obtain the individuals' consent before sharing their details with the researcher.

4.Suppose Beth contacts the individuals to ask their permission, and one-third of them respond giving permission, one-third respond denying permission, and one-third do not respond. Beth claims that at least one-half of the individuals are needed to make a valid study. What options are available to Beth? 

Answer: Beth has several options. She may proceed with the study using only the participants who granted permission, although this may reduce the validity of the research. She could attempt to contact the non-respondents again to obtain additional consent, provided this is ethically approved. Beth may also redesign the study to require a bigger sample size or use alternative sources of data. However, she should not include individuals who denied permission or assume that non-response implies consent. Respecting participants' choices and maintaining ethical research standards must remain a priority.

#### 2.5 Reflective Activity 1: Case study on inappropriate use of surveys
In 2018, Cambridge Analytica was in the news in the United Kingdom and the USA (Confessore, 2018) for obtaining and sharing data obtained from millions of Facebook users. They obtained the data through innocuous surveys on Facebook (you may have seen this type of survey and probably participated at times). This is probably the highest profile of surveys used for alternative means and, probably, monetary gains. However, this happens often through various media.
Consider how exactly this happened and why it was used.

There are other examples where, while the intention was not outward manipulation, the way the survey was conducted elicited different, and somehow, quite misleading results. A good example here is the case of the University of Michigan Consumer Sentiment Survey (2024–2025). Here, the researchers noted that a structural break occurred when the survey switched from phone to online administration, leading to significantly lower sentiment readings a drop estimated at around 8.9 index points, casting doubt on trend comparability and raising concerns about methodological integrity (a good analysis is available here:)

Read a good analysis of the University of Michigan Consumer Sentiment Survey.

Find one or two further examples of inappropriate use of surveys and highlight the impact of all these examples from the various ethical, social, legal and professional standpoints that apply.

Answer

1.The Literary Digest poll failure (1936)
   
What happened?

The magazine Literary Digest mailed approximately 10 million survey ballots and received about 2.4 million responses. The poll predicted a decisive victory for presidential candidate Alf Landon. Instead, Franklin D. Roosevelt won overwhelmingly. The survey relied heavily on telephone directories, automobile registrations, and similar lists that disproportionately represented wealthier citizens. Additionally, only a minority responded, creating severe nonresponse bias. 

Ethical issues

•	Failure to ensure a representative sample.

•	Publication of misleading conclusions despite methodological weaknesses.

Social impact

•	Misinformed the public regarding likely election outcomes.

•	Undermined confidence in polling and survey research.

Legal impact

•	No legal consequences, but it became a classic example of poor survey design.

Professional impact

•	Revolutionized survey methodology.

•	Demonstrated that sample quality matters more than sample size.

•	Led to advances in sampling theory and polling standards. 

2.Facebook Emotional Contagion Experiment (2012, disclosed 2014)

What happened?

Facebook manipulated the News Feeds of approximately 689,000 users without their explicit knowledge to study whether emotions could spread through social networks. Researchers altered the balance of positive and negative content shown to participants and measured subsequent posting behaviour.

Ethical issues

•	Absence of meaningful informed consent.

•	Use of human subjects without traditional ethical review.

•	Deliberate manipulation of emotional experiences.

Social impact

•	Public concern about the power of platforms to influence emotions and behaviour.

•	Increased scepticism regarding technology companies' use of personal data.

Legal impact

•	Exposed gaps between academic research ethics regulations and privately conducted digital studies. 

Professional impact

•	Triggered debates about ethical oversight in big-data research.

•	Encouraged calls for stronger ethical review processes for corporate research. 

Conclusion

These examples demonstrate that surveys are not ethically neutral tools. Whether through privacy violations, methodological flaws, or behavioural manipulation, poorly designed or improperly used surveys can have far-reaching consequences. From ethical, social, legal, and professional perspectives, the key principles that emerge are:

a)Informed consent must be respected

b)Survey methods must be transparent and scientifically valid.

c)Participants' privacy and independence must be protected.

d)Researchers and organizations must remain accountable for how survey data are collected, interpreted, and used.

Together, these cases illustrate why ethical survey practice is essential for maintaining public trust, producing reliable knowledge, and protecting individuals from misuse of their personal information.
